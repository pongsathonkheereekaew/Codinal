# 01 — EQ in Mastering

**Philosophy:** Mastering EQ is corrective, not creative. The mix is already made. EQ moves here are gentle (0.5–2dB typical, rarely >3dB) and broad.

## Yin and Yang — Interactive Perception (Katz)

| If you do this… | It perceptually feels like… |
|---|---|
| Dip at ~250Hz | Boost at ~5kHz |
| Add bass energy (50–80Hz) | Highs seem duller |
| Air boost (10kHz+) | Lower midrange seems thinner |
| Cut lower mids | Mix seems brighter and more open |

Use yin-yang: solve problems by cutting contrasting range, not boosting the deficit.

## Q / Bandwidth Selection

- Q 0.6–0.7: natural-sounding boosts; musical, no nasal artifacts
- Wide Q (0.4–0.6): gentle broad tonal shaping (preferred for mastering)
- Narrow Q (>2): surgical resonance removal only; do not boost with narrow Q
- Technique: boost wide → sweep to find resonance → narrow Q → dip

## EQ Feathering (Owsinski Ch.8)

To avoid boxiness or a hyped single-frequency artefact, **spread a boost across adjacent frequencies** instead of one focused Q.

Bad (boxy): `+3 dB at 100 Hz, Q 1.0`
Good (feathered): `+1.5 dB at 80 Hz, Q 0.7` AND `+1.5 dB at 120 Hz, Q 0.7`

The two combined achieve the same low-end weight without the resonance artefact of a single high-Q boost. The "felt" boost is broader and more musical.

⚡ Use for any move ≥ 2 dB on the master bus. Surgical cuts (problem removal) can stay single-band.

## Shelving EQ

- Standard shelf: sudden plateau — can sound artificial on full mixes
- **Baxandall curve**: preferred for air boosts — gradual rise from 10kHz to 20kHz, no sudden plateau; sounds more musical
- Warning: air boosts make ear perceive lower-midrange thinning; always check 200–800Hz after adding air

## High-pass / Low-pass

- High-pass: gentle roll-off on mixes with excessive sub-rumble; use gentle slope (6–12dB/oct); steep cuts can thin the mix
- Low-pass: rarely needed in mastering; only if harsh aliasing artifacts or intentional brightness reduction

## Linear-phase vs. Minimum-phase EQ

- Minimum-phase: colors depth, imaging; can smear transients; traditional analog character
- Linear-phase: sounds more analog-like (no phase smear), better stereo depth; potential pre-echo artifacts on high-Q moves
- Choose per-band: use linear-phase on broad moves, minimum-phase on surgical cuts

## Golden Rules of Mastering EQ

1. Never EQ in solo — always EQ in context of the full mix playing
2. Cut before you boost
3. If you can't fix it without affecting something else, the mix needs revision (→ redirect to Mixing)
4. Dull side is safer than too bright — over-bright masters fatigue listeners instantly
5. Check every EQ move in mono

## EQ Feathering (Owsinski)

Spread a boost across adjacent frequencies instead of a single large move.

**Example:**
- Instead of: +2dB at 100Hz
- Use: +1.5dB at 100Hz + 0.5dB at 80Hz + 0.5dB at 120Hz

**Why:** narrower individual boosts per band sound more natural; the ear perceives the combined effect as a broader gradual curve without boxiness or ringing.

**Mastering applications:**
- Bass weight: split between 60Hz + 80Hz; add small 120Hz shelf
- Low-mid warmth: 200Hz + 300Hz feathered, not single 250Hz
- Air: 10kHz + 12kHz + 16kHz feathered, not single 10kHz shelf

**Rule:** any mastering boost >1.5dB should be feathered across at least two adjacent frequencies. Mastering EQ moves are typically in tenths of dB; even 0.5dB can be audible on a full mix.
