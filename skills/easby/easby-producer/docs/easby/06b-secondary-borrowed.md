## 6. Secondary Dominants — Borrowed Tension

A secondary dominant is the V7 of a chord that isn't the tonic. Notated `V/X` — "five of X."

- **V/V** in C → D7 (the V of G). Common in country, gospel turnarounds.
- **V/vi** in C → E7 (the V of Am). The "Michelle" chord. Beatles. Instant cinematic.
- **V/IV** in C → C7. Same root as I but with a b7. Blues uses this constantly — I7 going to IV.
- **V/ii** in C → A7. Sets up the ii-V-I that defines jazz.

### Pop Irregular Resolutions

Pop breaks the classical rule that V/X must resolve to X. The dominant is treated as colour, not function.

- **V/vi → IV** instead of vi: "Creep" (Radiohead) — G–B–C–Cm. The B is V/vi (E minor) but goes to C.
- **V/IV held as colour**: any blues turnaround.
- **V/V → IV**: country pre-chorus move.

⚡ **The trick:** the chord still sounds like it's going somewhere, but you redirect it. Maximum forward energy, surprise destination.

## 7. Secondary Diminished Chords

Same idea as secondary dominants, different vehicle. A fully diminished 7th built a half-step below the target chord pulls strongly up to it.

- `#i°7 → ii` (e.g. C#°7 → Dm in C major)
- `#ii°7 → iii`
- `#iv°7 → V`

Common in jazz, gospel, ragtime. Chromatic stepwise bass line. One bar of dim7 then resolve — never sustain.

## 8. Borrowed Chords (Mode Mixture)

Pulling chords from the parallel minor (or major) into the home key. The harmonic equivalent of a sudden cloud.

| Chord (in C major) | Borrowed from | Emotional effect | Bass move |
|---|---|---|---|
| **iv** (Fm) | C minor | Sudden sadness over a familiar shape | F natural |
| **bVI** (Ab) | C minor | Cinematic warmth, "movie trailer" | Down to Ab |
| **bVII** (Bb) | C minor | Rock power, bypasses leading tone | Down whole step from C |
| **bIII** (Eb) | C minor | Dark, mysterious. Rare in pop, common in metal/score. | Down minor 3rd |
| **iiø7** (Dm7b5) | C minor | Anxious ii — sets up a more dramatic V | D natural |
| **Picardy 3rd** | Borrowed major I in a minor key | End on hope after sadness | Raises 3rd of i to I |

⚡ **iv after IV** (F → Fm in C) is the single most reliable emotional move in pop. The major-to-minor IV slide is in "Creep," "Blackbird," "Mad World," "While My Guitar Gently Weeps." Free tears, every time.

**Picardy 3rd** is the inverse: end a minor track on the major tonic. Works in cinematic outros and trad-folk. Use sparingly — once per track or it stops meaning anything.

## 9. Neapolitan + Augmented Sixth Chords — Dramatic / Chromatic Tools

These are the heavy artillery. Use one per section, not one per bar.

### Neapolitan (bII)

A major chord built on the flat 2nd, usually in first inversion (bII6). In C: Db/F.

- Function: predominant, leads to V or V7.
- Sound: chromatic, operatic, baroque drama.
- Use case: one bar before a half cadence to make the V feel inevitable.

### Augmented Sixth Chords

All resolve to V via two voices converging by half-step. The "wedge" gesture.

| Chord | In C, resolving to G | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| **Italian +6 (It+6)** | Ab–C–F# | Thin, archaic. |
| **French +6 (Fr+6)** | Ab–C–D–F# | Impressionist, Ravel-flavoured. |
| **German +6 (Ger+6)** | Ab–C–Eb–F# | Fattest, Wagnerian. Identical pitches to Ab7 — pivot point for modulation. |

⚡ The Ger+6 / dominant 7 ambiguity is gold: same audio, two functions. Use it as a hinge to swerve into a foreign key without preparation.
